# Paired paste overhead with and without imageAssets, and the Firefox tail, 2026-10-01

Two complete reference runs of the [paired protocol](../README.md) on the current
build passed their semantic and protocol checks for all 18 fixture, formatting and
browser combinations: 6,534 of 6,534 blocks each, no failures and no cleanup
failures. The first run uses the protocol as recorded on 2026-09-26; the second adds
`imageAssets: { mode: 'embedded' }` to the enabled route and an Image that accepts
embedded images to both routes (`--image-assets`). Every observed primary paired p95
stayed below the provisional 50 ms objective: 4.0 to 31.0 ms without `imageAssets`
and 6.0 to 31.0 ms with it. This is one local synthetic-input measurement, not a
product-wide latency bound.

Firefox again shows rare long dispatches, up to 873 ms. A profiled rerun traced them
to Firefox's cycle collector, which the engine runs synchronously inside the paste
task when an allocation-triggered garbage collection starts, and to the thread being
descheduled on a loaded machine. Neither is PasteCleanup's own work, and both also
hit the routes without PasteCleanup, but less often: the enabled route allocates more
per paste, and its dispatches reached 100 ms 1.8 times as often as those of each route
without PasteCleanup in the first run and 9 times as often with `imageAssets`. They
are recorded as a known limit below.

## The cost of imageAssets on a rich paste

The fixtures hold no images, so the second run measures what the clipboard asset
coordinator adds to an ordinary rich paste. It adds about 2 to 5 ms to the paired
p50 in Chromium and WebKit and 5 to 9 ms in Firefox, roughly one more normalization
of the same HTML (the standalone normalization p50 is 3 to 8 ms by fixture and
engine). That is what happens: with `imageAssets` the coordinator normalizes the
pasted HTML once to find image references, and when there are none it discards
that result and lets the ordinary paste route normalize the same HTML again.
Reusing the first result would remove the second pass; it is recorded as a
deferred improvement, since the p95 stays below the objective (at most 15.0 ms in
Chromium, 13.0 ms in WebKit and 31.0 ms in Firefox).

## The Firefox tail

Measured enabled Firefox dispatches of 100 ms or more: 46 of 7,200 without
`imageAssets` (0.64 percent, 5 of them 300 ms or more) and 52 of 7,200 with it
(0.72 percent, 18 of 300 ms or more). The routes without PasteCleanup had them too:
13 and 13 of 3,600 dispatches of 100 ms or more in the first run, up to 318 ms, and
3 and 3 in the second, up to 640 ms. Chromium and WebKit had none at or above 50 ms
in measured blocks; only their first pastes, the first dispatch in a fresh page,
reached 52 to 106 ms. The long dispatches come in bursts across routes, for
example blocks 50 to 57 of the first `rich-20k` preserve round.

**Trace.** The same bundle was replayed in the same Firefox for `rich-20k` preserve,
three fresh contexts of 121 blocks (2,904 paste dispatches), under the Gecko profiler
started from the environment (1 ms sampling, JavaScript and native stacks, thread
CPU use). A wrapper around `dispatchEvent` recorded each paste's absolute start and
duration and a user timing measure, so each long dispatch could be matched with the
samples and GC markers inside it. Profiling adds overhead; the trace's own
distribution was p50 31 ms, p95 42 ms and a maximum of 572 ms over all routes.

Nine dispatches took 100 ms or more:

- **Seven, 182 to 572 ms, were Firefox's cycle collector.** 82 to 95 percent of their
  samples are in the GC / CC category, almost all in `Incremental CC` with
  `CC (Build Graph)`, `CC (Collect White)` and `CC (Free Snow White)`, and the main
  thread used 97 percent of the wall time as CPU, so it was computing, not waiting.
  The collector runs synchronously from whatever JavaScript allocates at the moment
  an allocation-triggered major GC starts (each overlaps a `GCMajor` with reason
  `ALLOC_TRIGGER`): inside the cleanup parser (`parseBoundedHTML`), inside
  ProseMirror's view update, or at `document.createElement` in the table node view.
  What it collects is the DOM and JavaScript garbage of the editors the protocol
  creates and destroys, eight per block, so a page that does this continuously
  builds a large graph for each collection.
- **Two, 101 and 111 ms, were the thread descheduled.** Thread CPU was 41 and 40
  percent of their wall time, with no cycle collection and at most an 11 ms GC slice:
  the loaded machine took the core.

Of the 38 dispatches of 50 ms or more, 9 were dominated by the collector, 3 were
descheduled and 26 were ordinary work that ran slower under the profiler. Below
50 ms the thread used 96 percent of the wall time as CPU, and GC / CC took about
6 percent of the samples.

The enabled route had dispatches of 100 ms or more 1.8 times as often as each route
without PasteCleanup in the first run, and 9 times as often in the second, where it
normalizes the HTML twice. It allocates more per paste, cleanup's parse and then
ProseMirror's, and the collector lands wherever an allocation trips the trigger. A
page cannot postpone a collection the engine starts at an allocation; it runs inside
cleanup's synchronous parse and inside ProseMirror's synchronous update alike. Less
allocation per paste, or less DOM left as garbage, would make it rarer, not
impossible.

**Large first paste.** The 2026-09-28 large report recorded a first paste of
1,004 ms for `word-mixed-document` at 136 sections in Firefox. Four fresh contexts
under the profiler took 319, 311, 314 and 314 ms for the first paste and 216 to
249 ms for the next five. The first paste's extra time is cold code, baseline
compilation and interpreted frames, and minor GCs; none had a cycle collection.
The 1,004 ms did not recur; the mechanism above or the machine load of that run
would explain it, but this trace does not prove which.

**Known limit.** In Firefox, an individual paste can take several hundred
milliseconds longer when the engine's cycle collector runs inside it, as above, or
when the machine is loaded. In this protocol it happened to 0.6 to 0.7 percent of
enabled dispatches at 100 ms or more, up to 873 ms, and the routes without
PasteCleanup had such dispatches too, at a rate 1.8 to 9 times lower. The p95
objective is unaffected, but it does not bound single pastes. The support matrix and
the PasteCleanup README say so.

## Limitations

- Synthetic inputs and one machine, under the background load listed above.
- The protocol creates and destroys an editor for every operation, which builds
  more DOM garbage than an application with one long-lived editor; how often the
  collector lands in a paste in such an application was not measured.
- The trace replays one fixture and policy with the profiler running; its timings
  are higher than the unprofiled runs.
- The raw `samples.jsonl` files stay in local scratch: 24,056,907 bytes, SHA-256
  `f31ec5737c6055e4bae84e8fa382127609bbfe188d1e8dbb6f4ab3ee527e238a`, without
  `imageAssets`, and 24,062,617 bytes, SHA-256
  `bfd1a5279e6a393b7770ac9a2f05745f75dd6dadc0103a54b38f729c4192701e`, with it. The
  committed JSON files equal each run's `report.json` byte for byte. The Gecko
  profiles (301 MB and 26 MB) are not kept; the protocol and the trace method permit
  an independent rerun.
